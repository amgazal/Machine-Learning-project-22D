# Detecting AI-Generated Tweets
**AI4ALL — Group 22D**

*Can we classify between human vs. AI-generated tweets?*

---

## The Problem

An estimated 20%+ of accounts on X (formerly Twitter) are believed to be bots, and even conservative academic estimates put automated accounts at 9–15% of all users — tens of millions of accounts. As generative AI models have gotten better at producing convincing, human-sounding text, it's become increasingly difficult to know whether the content you're reading was written by a real person or generated by AI at scale.

### Why it matters
AI-generated text at scale can be used to simulate authentic public conversation, amplify coordinated influence campaigns, and spread misinformation faster than it can be fact-checked. Being able to flag likely AI-generated content matters for researchers studying discourse, journalists verifying sources, and platforms trying to maintain trust with their users.

### Our research question
**How can the process of creating AI/ML solutions amplify or mitigate bias in the case our group is using?**

We explore this through the lens of a concrete task: building a model to classify tweets as human-written or AI-generated, and examining where bias enters — and can be caught — throughout that process.

---

## The Dataset

We combined two public datasets into one benchmark of **35,443 tweets**:

| Source | Description | Rows (cleaned) |
|---|---|---|
| [TweepFake](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0251415) | General human-vs-bot tweet benchmark; bot tweets generated via Markov chains, RNN, LSTM, and GPT-2 | 25,553 |
| [ElectAI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16116) | Election-claims authorship dataset; AI tweets generated via Falcon, LLaMA, and Mistral | 9,890 |
| **Combined** | | **35,443** (44.2% human / 55.8% AI-generated) |

TweepFake alone would only teach a model to catch older, simpler bots. ElectAI adds modern LLM output and grounds the task in a real civic-integrity use case — combining both makes the model less dependent on any single generator's quirks.

**Data quality:** 0 missing values, 0 duplicate tweets after cleaning (case/whitespace-insensitive).

---

## Exploratory Data Analysis

Before modeling, we checked the data's balance, cleanliness, and length patterns.

- **Class balance:** 15,647 human (44.2%) vs. 19,796 AI-generated (55.8%) — close enough to model directly, though we still applied `class_weight="balanced"` during training as a safeguard.
- **Source × label breakdown:** TweepFake is nearly 50/50 (12,781 human / 12,772 AI). **ElectAI is heavily AI-skewed** (2,866 human / 7,024 AI) — a fact that turns out to matter a great deal later.
- **Tweet length:** AI-generated tweets run slightly longer on average (median 22 words vs. 16 for human), with substantial overlap between classes.

## The Models

We trained and evaluated three models, all using the same stratified 80/20 train-test split of the merged dataset:

| Model | Type | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Logistic Regression** | Supervised, TF-IDF features | Tweet → TF-IDF vectorization → linear decision boundary → Human or AI. Our baseline: simple, fast, interpretable. |
| **Random Forest** | Supervised, TF-IDF features | Tweet → TF-IDF features → 100 decision trees → majority vote → Human or AI. Captures more complex, non-linear relationships than a single linear model. |
| **BERT (fine-tuned)** | Supervised, transformer | Raw tweet → tokenizer → BERT transformer → classification layer → Human or AI. Understands language context and word order, not just word/phrase patterns. |

**Limitations of each:** Logistic Regression assumes a linear boundary and struggles with complex language patterns. Random Forest still depends on handcrafted TF-IDF features and doesn't understand word order. BERT needs no manual feature engineering and captures context, but is the most computationally expensive and, as we found, the most capable of learning shortcuts if they exist in the data.

## Key Findings

| Model | Accuracy | Precision | Recall | F1 | AUC-ROC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Logistic Regression | 82.35% | 83.30% | 85.55% | 0.844 | 0.919 |
| Random Forest | 82.37% | 80.79% | 89.77% | 0.850 | — |
| **BERT** | **92.09%** | **92.20%** | **93.76%** | **0.930** | — |

*[Insert: grouped bar chart comparing Accuracy/Precision/Recall/F1 across all three models]*

BERT outperformed both classical models across every metric. But the most important finding came from digging deeper than the aggregate numbers: breaking Random Forest's performance down **by source dataset** revealed a large generalization gap.

| Source | Accuracy | F1 |
|---|---|---|
| ElectAI | 98.1% | 0.987 |
| TweepFake | 76.3% | 0.781 |

The model's top feature by importance was the literal string `https`, followed by election-topic words like `county`, `vote`, and `voter fraud`. Because ElectAI is mostly AI-labeled and entirely election-focused, the model had partly learned **"is this an election tweet"** as a proxy for **"is this AI-generated"** — a topic/label correlation introduced by how we merged the datasets, not a real writing-style signal. This means our headline accuracy numbers overstate how well the models actually detect AI *writing style*, independent of topic.

## Limitations, Future Work, and Ethical Considerations

**Limitations**
- Large accuracy gap between source datasets (98% on ElectAI vs. 76% on TweepFake) shows part of our models' performance comes from topic artifacts, not pure AI-vs-human writing style.
- False positives (human tweets flagged as AI) and false negatives (AI tweets flagged as human) both occur, and a classifier's mistakes could unfairly cast doubt on a real person's writing.
- Model performance may vary across writing styles, languages, and demographics not well represented in our two source datasets.

**Ethical considerations**
- **Privacy:** tweet text can contain personal or sensitive information; our models operate on public text only.
- **Misuse risk:** a classifier like this could be misused to unfairly judge or penalize individuals based on writing style rather than actual authorship.
- **Positive applications:** bot/AI-account detection for platforms, filtering AI-generated content before academic analysis, helping journalists verify authentic public conversation, and supporting misinformation research.

**Next steps**
- Strip URLs and topic-identifying tokens (e.g. `https`, election-specific terms) before retraining, to test how much accuracy is really style-based.
- Test generalization by training on one source dataset and evaluating on the other, rather than only a random split across both.
- Collect more diverse, multilingual tweet data to reduce topic and language bias.

---

## Impact

Beyond the classifier itself, this project is a case study in **how bias enters an ML pipeline through dataset choices, and how disaggregated evaluation — not aggregate accuracy — is what surfaces it.** Merging two well-intentioned datasets created a hidden topic/label correlation that a single overall accuracy score would have completely hidden; only breaking results down by source exposed it. That's the core, evidence-based answer to our essential question: responsible AI/ML practice isn't just about the training step — it's about actively looking for the shortcuts a model might be exploiting before trusting its numbers.
